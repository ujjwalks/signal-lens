# Translating seller language into buyer language

> **Not loaded by the skill.** This was `references/translation.md` until an isolation
> test measured it. Its two signature contributions — mechanics-derived phrasing and the
> inverted gap — scored **identically** with and without it (100/100 and 60/60), so it was
> not earning the tokens it cost on every run. The parts that measurably mattered are now
> inline in `SKILL.md`; the rest is kept here because the four-failure-mode analysis is
> good research and the next person to touch this should read it before re-adding anything.

The seller writes *"automated multi-entity consolidation."* The buyer writes
*"I paste the trial balance into a master workbook and the mapping tab broke again."*
Nothing on the website contains the second sentence, and the second sentence is what you
are searching for.

Get this wrong and every signal downstream is wrong, because you will be searching for
words only the seller uses.

## Four ways the translation fails

Only the first is the one people expect.

**1. Vocabulary gap.** The seller names a capability; the buyer names an artifact and a
verb performed on it. "Duct remediation" → *"upstairs is ten degrees hotter and my bill
was $487."* Derive the buyer's phrasing from **materials and mechanics, not marketing**:
MDF creeps under static load at 18–40 months, so the buyer types *"there's a dip where my
keyboard sits."* A non-braced frame racks at full extension, so they type *"it does a
little dance."* Neither sentence is reachable by expanding synonyms of the seller's copy.

**2. Inverted gap — the dangerous one.** The seller's promise and the buyer's stated ask
are *opposed*. An observability vendor sells "keep all your data at full fidelity"; the
buyer is asking how to keep **less**. Search on the seller's language and you retrieve the
happy, high-spending half of the market. The real translation is that *"I want to store
less"* means *"I was forced to trade fidelity for price"* — which is the same problem,
stated as its own opposite.

**3. No gap at all.** Sometimes the buyer is fluent — they say *MER*, *blended CAC*,
*I-140 EB2 NIW, RFE on prong 2, filed pro se*. Here the usual test ("if it could appear on
the seller's site it is wrong") under-fires, because the buyer and the seller share
vocabulary. What has to be translated instead is **posture** — the sound of someone
auditing a decision they have already made: *"am I being farmed"*, *"is this normal"*,
*"talk me out of it"* — and **side**, because identical sentences come from the supply side
of the same market.

**4. Prohibited bridge.** The most persuasive true sentence the seller could say is one
they are not allowed to say — a health claim, an outcome guarantee, a legal assurance. The
bridge itself is barred, so a third register has to be built in advance out of verifiable
product facts that answer the question without touching the claim.

## The five artifacts

Derive all five **before** writing a single signal.

### A. Workaround inventory, artifact-first

For each way buyers cope today, name three things:

- **The artifact** — a concrete noun the buyer can see, open or photograph. The workbook.
  The Thanos compactor. Ads Manager at 11pm. The 47-therapist PDF. The stack of Amazon
  boxes. The folder of unwatched courses.
- **The verb** they perform on it weekly.
- **The failure mode and time to failure.**

The artifact is the only object both sides can see, and detection anchors on it.

### B. Competitor set, by name, in three tiers

1. **Direct paid products.**
2. **The free or manual substitute** — usually the true incumbent. Excel. Indeed.
   YouTube. Self-filing. "Just let the algorithm cook."
3. **The person they pay to do it** — the CPA firm billing $2k/mo, the freelance media
   buyer, the fractional CFO, the consultant on Fiverr.

Tier 3 never appears on a website and is a paid competitor in half of all cases.
**Complaints naming any tier are the most reliable signal available**, and you cannot find
them without the names.

### C. The countable severity noun

Exactly one noun whose count decides whether a post is worth nothing or five figures:
legal entities, GB ingested per month, monthly ad spend, doors under management, tonnage
plus metro, employees plus self-insured status, priority date, hours available per week.

Derive it explicitly and require it. **This is what stops the engine ordering the market
backwards by pain intensity** — the most emotionally vivid posts come from the smallest
accounts.

Pick the noun precisely: *entities with payroll*, not *entities that exist*. "40 clients"
from a solo bookkeeper serving single-member LLCs is not forty consolidations.

### D. Vocabulary split test

Write the same complaint twice — once credentialed (*"due to / due from won't tie out"*),
once lay (*"I just want one number for all my businesses"*).

**If the two share tokens, you have not found the lay register.** Index both. They need
opposite replies: the expert wants to know whether you handle FX translation and minority
interest; the lay buyer needs to be told the category exists.

### E. Prohibited-bridge test

Write the most persuasive true sentence the seller could say. Ask whether they are
permitted to say it. Where they are not, define the third register in advance —
verifiable, checkable facts (form, dose, published lot COA, one-click cancel, flat fee, no
job guarantee, licence footprint) that answer the question without touching the barred
claim. Block the claim class in the generator rather than prompting against it.

## Validation

Reconstruct twenty posts the seller's five best customers would plausibly have written in
the ninety days before they bought. If your derived signals do not catch most of them, the
translation is wrong — not the signal list.

Then invert it: for each signal, write the post that looks identical and means the
opposite. If you cannot, you have not understood the signal well enough to search for it.
